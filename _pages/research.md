My work examines **pragmatic inference as a mechanism for social learning**. When a speaker could have said one thing but said another, listeners — including young children — can reason about *why*. I study how this kind of reasoning lets children learn things no one explicitly told them.

## Explanation as a cue to the unexpected

People tend to explain things that are surprising. If a teacher says *why* a child did something, does that tell other children the behavior was unusual? I use show-and-tell and conversational paradigms to test how explanation utterances shape children's inferences about what is normal, expected, and appropriate.

## Effort praise and inferences about ability

Praise like "you worked so hard!" is meant to encourage — but it can also imply that the task was hard *for that person*. I study when and how children draw inferences about ability from effort-focused feedback, and how these inferences compare to other cues such as surprise.

## Explanations and social groups

How information is communicated can shape beliefs about groups, not just individuals. This line of work examines how the form of an explanation — and who gives it to whom — influences children's and adults' inferences about gender and other social categories, including how people judge their own and others' understanding.

## Methods

I use behavioral experiments with children and adults, conducted in the lab and online, and analyze data with mixed-effects models in R. Study materials are shared in my [stimuli bank on GitHub](https://github.com/rose-reagan/exp_stimuli_bank).
