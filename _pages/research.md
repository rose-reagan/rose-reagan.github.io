My work examines **pragmatic inference as a mechanism for social learning**. When a speaker could have said one thing but said another, listeners — including young children — can reason about *why*. I study how this kind of reasoning lets children learn things no one explicitly told them.

All of the studies below are preregistered.

## Explanation as a cue to the unexpected

People tend to explain things that are surprising. If a teacher explains *why* a child brought something to class, do other children conclude that the thing is unusual?

**Show and Tell, Study 1.** *Does explaining something make it seem weird?* Children hear a teacher respond to classmates' show-and-tell items in one of three ways: by explaining the item ("Let me tell you why he brought this"), celebrating it ("It's great that he brought this"), or simply acknowledging it ("Okay, he brought this"). We test whether an explanation leads children to judge the item as less normal, and less likely to be something other kids would bring, than the other two responses.
<br>*Children ages 6–10 · within-subjects design*

**Show and Tell, Study 2.** *Does it matter whether the teacher explains everyone's item, or just one?* Building on Study 1, children see two classrooms: one where the teacher explains only one child's item, and one where she explains every child's item. We test whether being singled out for explanation makes an item seem stranger, and explore whether it also affects how popular that child seems and whether children want to be their friend.
<br>*Children ages 6–10, plus an adult comparison sample · within-subjects design*

## Praise and inferences about ability

Praise like "You worked so hard!" is meant to encourage. But when everyone does equally well and only one person is praised for effort, the praise can imply that the task was harder *for that person*.

**Pragmatic Contrast Study.** *Do children infer that a classmate praised for effort is less able?* A teacher singles out one student for effort praise even though every student performed the same. In one version the task is math, where effort could signal ability; in the other it's cleaning up, where it shouldn't. We test whether children judge the praised student as less smart, and as needing more study and help, only when the task is academic, and at what age this inference emerges.
<br>*Children ages 5–10 · within-subjects design*

**Surprise vs. Praise.** *Are some cues to low ability easier to read than others?* Children see two equally successful students, and the teacher directs either effort praise or an expression of surprise toward one of them. Children either judge which student is smarter from the teacher's words, or predict which student the teacher would say it to. We expect surprise to be easier to interpret than praise, and predicting speech to be easier than working backward from it.
<br>*Children ages 6–12 · within-subjects design*

## Explanations and social groups

How information is explained can shape beliefs about entire groups, not just individuals.

**Zarpies.** *Does the way we explain one group member's behavior shape beliefs about the whole group?* Participants learn about a novel social group, the Zarpies, and hear behaviors explained either as something about the group ("because Zarpies like her do that") or as something about the individual ("because she has always liked that"). We test whether group-based explanations lead people to generalize behaviors to more group members, see them as more normal for the group, and spontaneously explain new behaviors in group terms.
<br>*Between-subjects design · N = 120*

## Explaining, understanding, and gender

People often believe they understand how everyday things work far better than they do. This gap, the *illusion of explanatory depth*, typically appears once they try to explain. I study how this illusion plays out when people explain things to someone else, and whether it differs by gender.

**Illusion of Explanatory Depth, Studies 1–3.** *Does the illusion hold up in social settings, and does gender shape it?* Adults rate how well they understand everyday devices, then try to explain how they work, and rate themselves again. Study 1 replicates the classic paradigm (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002) with written explanations. Study 2 moves the explanations into a live conversation with an experimenter. Study 3 places them in a more natural back-and-forth with a partner and measures how people explain, including how long they talk and how often they hedge. Across studies, we explore whether the illusion differs by the gender of the explainer and of the listener.
<br>*Adults · repeated-measures designs · N = 90–100 per study*

## Methods

I use behavioral experiments with children and adults, conducted in the lab and online, and analyze data with mixed-effects models in R. Study materials are shared in my [stimuli bank on GitHub](https://github.com/rose-reagan/exp_stimuli_bank).
