My work examines **language as a mechanism for social learning**, with a special focus on **pragmatic inference**. When a speaker could have said one thing but instead said another, listeners (including the littlest ones!) can reason about *why*. I study how this kind of reasoning lets children learn things no one explicitly told them.

Here's a glimpse of some of what I'm working on:

## Explanation as a cue to (ab)normality

We tend to explain things that are unusual; after all, if something was normal or unsurprising, why would it merit an explanation? In a line of studies with children ages 6-12 years, we ask: if a teacher explains *why* a child brought something to class for show and tell, do other children conclude that it's unusual?

## Praise and inferences about ability

Effort praise, such as "You worked so hard!", is meant to encourage. But when everyone does equally well and only one child is praised for effort, do children conclude the task was harder *for that child*?

## Explanations and social groups

How we explain one person's behavior can shape beliefs about their whole social group. If a behavior is explained as something about the group rather than the individual, do people assume the whole group does it?

## Explaining, understanding, and gender

People often think they understand how everyday things work better than they do, until they try to explain them. I study how this *illusion of explanatory depth* plays out when people explain things to each other, and whether it differs by gender.
